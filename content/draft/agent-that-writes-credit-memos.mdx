## What does a credit memo agent actually need to do?

A credit memo is not a summary. It is a structured risk argument — typically 15 to 60 pages for corporate lending — built from financial statements, management accounts, sector data, covenant history, and credit policy rules. For large corporate clients, Evalueserve (2026) documents memos running 50–60 pages, with production cycles so rigid they cannot scale during peak underwriting periods.

The agent needs to do four things: pull the right data, interpret it against the borrower's risk profile, draft a memo that a credit committee will accept, and do all of this in a way that a regulator can audit.

None of these steps is optional. Skip data governance and you get an inaccurate memo. Skip the draft structure and you get output a committee rejects. Skip the audit trail and you have an EU AI Act compliance problem.

## The four-stage pipeline anatomy

The framework I use for this is what I call **The Credit Memo Stack** — four stages that any builder designing an AI credit memo automation pipeline can use as a design reference.

The table below shows each stage, what the agent handles, where the human stays in the loop, and which stack element carries the work.

| Stage | What the agent does | Human role | Stack element |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ingest | Pulls financials, bureau data, management accounts, prior covenants from connected sources | Confirms data completeness before analysis begins | n8n · Supabase Storage |
| Analyse | Runs ratio analysis, trend detection, sector benchmarking, covenant headroom calculation against a vectorised policy library | Reviews flagged anomalies | Claude API · pgvector |
| Draft | Structures the memo sections, writes risk narrative, populates tables, inlines citations linked to source documents | Edits and approves narrative — cannot delegate | Claude API |
| Review gate | Routes draft to credit analyst queue with change-tracking; logs final approved version with timestamps | Mandatory sign-off — non-delegable by design | Supabase · n8n |

The Review gate is not a UX feature. It is an architectural requirement. Under the EU AI Act, credit analysis AI falls into the high-risk category — and "human oversight" must be demonstrable, not just claimed.

## The EU AI Act compliance design you cannot skip

Credit scoring and credit analysis AI is classified as high-risk under Annex III, point 5(b) of the EU AI Act. Compliance obligations were due August 2026, with possible extension to December 2027 for standalone Annex III systems — but the design requirements apply regardless of when enforcement lands (EU AI Act / Gibson Dunn, May 2026). Non-compliance carries fines up to €30M.

What a compliant credit memo agent requires:

**Audit trail by design.** Every data source used in a memo must be logged with its retrieval timestamp and version. Supabase with row-level audit logging handles this cleanly — each memo generation event writes a complete provenance record before the draft exists.

**Explainability at source level.** The agent's citations must link to the specific document excerpt that generated a risk observation. pgvector retrieval with passage-level metadata makes this achievable; a memo that says "revenue declined 12% YoY" without the source excerpt is not compliant — and will not survive credit committee scrutiny either.

**Human oversight that is auditable.** The Review gate is not a checkbox. The compliance design requires that a qualified human reviewed the output and has a timestamped record of that review. Building this into the workflow architecture from day one is cheaper than retrofitting it later.

Compliance is not overhead here. It is the architecture.

3. **Claude API for drafting, not just summarising.** The distinction matters. A summary repackages inputs. A draft makes an argument. The prompt engineering for the Draft stage needs to encode the memo's rhetorical structure — executive summary, financial analysis, risk factors, recommendation — not just instruct the model to "write a credit memo."
