The EU AI Act's maximum fine for non-compliant high-risk AI systems is €15 million or 3% of global annual revenue — whichever is higher. (EU AI Act, Article 71, 2024) Annex III enforcement began August 2, 2026. (EU AI Act, Annex III, 2024) If your firm's AI systems were live before that date without compliant logging in place, you are already exposed.

The harder problem: a gap in a log cannot be filled retroactively. A regulator requesting 6 months of records will find either the records or the absence — and the absence is the finding.

## What does EU AI Act Article 13 actually require for audit trails?

**Article 13 establishes transparency and documentation obligations for high-risk AI systems — it is the interpretive layer on top of the Article 12 event log. (EU AI Act, Article 13, 2024)** Article 12 mandates automatic recording of every AI operation. Article 13 requires providers to ensure those logs are comprehensible to deployers: what was captured, what anomalies to look for, and how to reconstruct the decision pathway from the record.

Together, they define what I call the **Traceability Stack** — 4 layers that must all be present before a log is compliant under both articles.

The following table maps each layer to its financial services application:

| Layer | What it captures | Financial services example |
|---|---|---|
| Event | AI operation, input, output, timestamp, model version | Credit decision: score, model ID, request ID |
| Attribution | Named user or process that directed the call | Underwriter ID who initiated the AML screen |
| Context | Prompt version, system instructions hash, data source vintage | Credit model training data cutoff date |
| Oversight | Human review gate, override decision, escalation with reasoning | Underwriter override of AI rejection with documented reason |

A log that captures only the Event layer satisfies Article 12 in its narrowest reading. A log that captures all 4 layers satisfies the full scope of Articles 12 and 13 — and provides what a supervisory authority will actually look for in a review.

## Why do most financial services firms have a compliance gap right now?

**The most common gap is not missing logs — it is missing attribution.** Most enterprise AI deployments route model calls through a shared service account or API key. The log records that the call was made. It does not record which person directed it. (Kognitos, AI Audit Trail Requirements 2026)

GDPR, DORA, and MiFID II all require individual attribution for decisions that affect data subjects or financial outcomes. (GDPR, Article 5(1)(a), 2018; DORA, Article 8(4), 2022; MiFID II, Article 25, 2014) A credit decision routed through a shared service account has no attributable human — which means no compliant audit trail, regardless of how detailed the event log is.

The second gap is prompt versioning. A log that records inputs and outputs but not which version of the system instructions the model was operating under is incomplete. A credit scoring AI running on last month's prompt configuration and today's produces different decisions. Without version control on the prompt layer, the log cannot reconstruct the decision context a regulator needs to evaluate.

The third gap is timing. Article 12 requires deployers of high-risk AI systems to retain logs for a minimum of 6 months — 24 months for biometric and law enforcement systems. (EU AI Act, Article 12, 2024) Firms that began logging at the August 2 enforcement date will reach 6 months of compliant records in February 2027. Firms that began building their logging architecture in early 2025 arrived at enforcement day with records ready.

## How to build the Traceability Stack in a regulated financial services environment

**The implementation sequence that works in regulated environments:**

Start with event capture in EU-sovereign infrastructure. For EU-based financial services firms, logs must reside in EU-jurisdiction compute — not merely EU-region storage on a US-headquartered platform. For AI systems processing EU personal data, routing inference through a non-EU API endpoint constitutes an international data transfer requiring appropriate safeguards — an adequacy decision, standard contractual clauses, or equivalent. (GDPR, Articles 44-49, 2018) Storing output logs in EU infrastructure while inference runs on a non-EU endpoint does not resolve the transfer requirement. Architect both inference and logging to remain in EU-jurisdiction compute to eliminate the transfer risk entirely.

In practice: Supabase (EU Frankfurt region) for the event log, with Row-Level Security policies enforcing access controls by role, and a dedicated `ai_decision_log` table capturing request\_id, model\_id, model\_version, prompt\_hash, input\_hash, output\_summary, user\_id, session\_id, human\_reviewed (boolean), override\_reason, and timestamp at the point of inference.

Wire attribution at the session layer, not the AI layer. Attribution must be captured before the AI call is made — in the session that initiated it. In an n8n workflow, this means passing the initiating user ID from the trigger node through to the logging node explicitly. If it is not passed explicitly, it is not logged. The AI model cannot infer who directed the work.

Version-control your system instructions. Each system instruction set gets a SHA hash or version tag, committed to a repository, and that version identifier is recorded with every model call. This allows a log entry from 6 months ago to be replayed in the exact prompt context it was created.

Build the human oversight gate as a structured log entry. For credit decisions, AML flags, or onboarding risk scores — every human review, approval, and override must generate a structured record at the moment it occurs: decision (approve / override / escalate), reviewer ID, reasoning (free text, minimum character floor), and timestamp. A comment in a spreadsheet is not a log entry. A structured database row with a foreign key to the AI decision record is.

## What I would do

1. **Audit every production AI system against the 4-layer Traceability Stack.** For each system, answer: do we have all 4 layers? Where is the gap? This takes 1 working day per system with infrastructure access — and surfaces the highest-risk gaps immediately.

2. **Fix attribution before the next supervisory cycle.** If any production AI system routes calls through a shared service account, reroute through user-specific tokens or pass user context explicitly through the call chain within 30 days. This is the most common finding in AI compliance reviews and the fastest to fix architecturally.

3. **Set up a dedicated `ai_decision_log` table in EU-sovereign infrastructure.** The schema above covers the Article 12 event capture requirement. Adding the prompt\_hash and model\_version columns is the difference between a log and a compliant audit record.

4. **Implement prompt versioning before the next system instruction update.** Every change to a system instruction set increments a version number, commits the diff to version control, and records the running version with every inference call. This is a one-day implementation change with permanent compliance value.

5. **Run a simulated regulatory review now.** Pull 3 months of existing logs and attempt to reconstruct a credit decision end-to-end: who initiated it, what data was used, which version of the model and prompt ran, what the output was, whether a human reviewed it, and what they decided. If you cannot reconstruct the decision from the log, a supervisor cannot either — and that is the test.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does EU AI Act Article 13 require for audit trails in financial services?**
Article 13 requires that high-risk AI systems are sufficiently transparent that deployers can interpret the system's outputs and the event logs generated during operation. For financial services firms, this means providers must supply instructions explaining what the Article 12 log captures and how to reconstruct a decision from it. Both the log and the interpretive documentation must be available to national supervisory authorities on request.

**How long must AI audit logs be retained under the EU AI Act?**
Article 12 requires deployers of high-risk AI systems to retain logs for a minimum of 6 months. For biometric identification and law enforcement AI systems, the retention period is 24 months. These are minimums — DORA and MiFID II impose additional documentation and retention requirements for financial entities, and firms should apply the longest applicable period across all overlapping regimes.

**What is the most common AI audit trail compliance gap in financial services?**
Missing individual attribution. When AI model calls route through a shared service account or API key, the log captures the call but not the person who directed it. GDPR, DORA, and MiFID II all require individual attribution for AI decisions affecting data subjects or financial outcomes. A credit decision with no named human in the log chain has no compliant audit trail regardless of how complete the event record is.

**What happens if a regulated firm cannot produce an AI audit trail when requested?**
Under the EU AI Act, failure to maintain compliant documentation for high-risk AI systems carries fines up to €15 million or 3% of global annual revenue, whichever is higher. Under DORA, which applies to all financial entities in the EU from January 2025, failure to maintain adequate ICT risk documentation — which regulators increasingly interpret to include AI decision logs — carries additional supervisory action by competent national authorities including the ECB, EBA, and national NCAs.
