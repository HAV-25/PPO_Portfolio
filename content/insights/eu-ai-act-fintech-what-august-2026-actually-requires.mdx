The European Banking Authority's November 2025 report on AI Act implications found that the majority of AI use cases at EBA-supervised institutions fall into the high-risk classification (EBA, November 2025). Not experimental systems. Not proof-of-concept pilots. The production AI running credit scoring, fraud detection, and AML profiling at most regulated firms.

That is the compliance exposure. The headline deadline — August 2, 2026 — was when those obligations were scheduled to become fully enforceable. Then the Digital Omnibus changed the timeline. Here is what actually changed, what did not, and what a fintech operator needs to do in the window that remains.

## What did the Digital Omnibus actually change about the August 2026 deadline?

The EU institutions reached a provisional political agreement on the Digital Omnibus — a package amending the EU AI Act — in early 2026. The result: high-risk obligations for stand-alone Annex III systems are deferred from August 2, 2026 to December 2, 2027 (Gibson Dunn, January 2026). For AI embedded in regulated Annex I products, the deadline moves further, to August 2, 2028.

Annex III is where most fintech AI sits: credit scoring, AML risk profiling, fraud detection, loan approval, insurance underwriting, and biometric identification. The Omnibus bought 16 additional months for these systems. This looks like breathing room. It is actually a longer runway to do the same amount of work — and some obligations were already overdue before the Omnibus was signed.

## What is already in force and non-negotiable?

The Omnibus deferred high-risk compliance. It did not defer everything. Two obligations were already in force from February 2, 2025:

**AI literacy (Article 4):** Every organisation must ensure that staff using or overseeing AI systems have sufficient AI literacy. If you have not run a documented, role-differentiated AI literacy programme, you have been out of compliance since February 2025 (EU AI Act, Article 4). Regulators will check this first — it is the cheapest obligation to demonstrate and the most visible to an auditor.

**Prohibited practices:** Article 5 prohibitions are active. Real-time remote biometric surveillance in public spaces, AI that exploits psychological vulnerabilities, and social scoring by public authorities are banned outright. AML and fraud detection systems that use behavioural pattern scoring need to be checked against this list — not as a paperwork exercise, but as a design review.

DORA — the Digital Operational Resilience Act — has been fully applicable since January 17, 2025. DORA requires incident logging, operational resilience testing, and third-party ICT risk management. The overlap with EU AI Act Article 12 (record-keeping) and Article 14 (human oversight) is not incidental. Building AI Act compliance infrastructure in isolation from DORA is creating duplicated work.

## What does high-risk AI compliance actually require in financial services?

High-risk systems under Annex III must meet six core obligation categories under Chapter III of the Act. Here is what each requires for a financial services operator:

| Obligation | Article | What it requires in fintech |
|---|---|---|
| Risk management | Art. 9 | Continuous testing and documentation across the system lifecycle — not a one-time audit |
| Data governance | Art. 10 | Training data examined for bias; data provenance documented; ongoing drift monitoring |
| Technical documentation | Art. 11 | Full system spec, intended purpose, performance metrics — machine-readable and audit-ready |
| Record-keeping | Art. 12 | Automatic operation logs sufficient to trace any consequential AI-assisted decision |
| Transparency | Art. 13 | Users informed when AI is used; meaningful explanations available on request |
| Human oversight | Art. 14 | Override and stop capability designed in; escalation paths documented and tested |

A financial institution with 5–10 high-risk AI systems typically takes 12–18 months from inventory to conformity assessment (Finextra, June 2026). December 2027 is the deadline. That means the compliance build window for most firms opened in mid-2026 at the latest. The Omnibus did not reduce the scope — it adjusted the delivery date.

## How does EU AI Act compliance intersect with GDPR Article 22 and DORA?

High-risk AI in fintech frequently overlaps with GDPR Article 22, which restricts solely automated decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects. Credit scoring and loan approval are the canonical examples.

The overlap creates a dual compliance obligation. GDPR Article 22 requires an opt-out from purely automated decisions and a right to human review. EU AI Act Article 14 requires override and stop capability. The technical implementation is similar — a human review queue, an override audit trail, a documented escalation path — but the documentation and notification requirements are distinct frameworks with different authorities checking them.

Firms building GDPR Article 22 compliance that does not also satisfy AI Act Article 12 logging requirements will need to retrofit. The correct approach: build the logging layer once, map it to both frameworks' output schemas, and avoid the rework.

For DORA: the Article 12 record-keeping requirement and DORA's incident log requirement both demand timestamped, queryable decision records. An n8n-based audit pipeline writing to Supabase with row-level security can satisfy both simultaneously — one infrastructure layer, two output schemas. Building separately is waste.

## The 4-phase compliance build

With December 2027 as the live deadline and 12–18 months as the build timeline, the window for starting is now, not mid-2027. The 4-Phase Compliance Build is the sequence that works:

**Phase 1 — Inventory (Q3 2026):** Enumerate every AI system in production, staging, and third-party integrations that has decision-making capability. You cannot classify risk on systems you have not counted. The EBA has signalled that national competent authorities expect supervised institutions to demonstrate measurable progress by mid-2026 (EBA, November 2025). The inventory is the first piece of evidence.

**Phase 2 — Classification (Q4 2026):** Map each system against Annex III categories using the EU AI Office's published guidance, not internal risk intuition. Credit scoring is high-risk even with a 97% accuracy rate and no customer complaints. Classification is based on intended use, not performance.

**Phase 3 — Documentation (Q1–Q2 2027):** Build Article 9–13 compliance artefacts — risk management documentation, data governance records, technical documentation, operation logs. Treat this as product documentation, not a compliance overhead. Systems documented this way are easier to operate, audit, and hand over.

**Phase 4 — Conformity (Q3 2027):** Conformity assessment ahead of the December 2027 deadline. Internal for most Annex III systems; third-party assessment required for some. Build the Article 14 human oversight mechanism as a production feature — a review queue, an override trail, a tested escalation path — not a compliance bolt-on that exists on paper only.

## What I would do

1. **Run the inventory now.** Cross-functional — legal, engineering, operations, compliance. Include third-party AI components where your firm is the deployer. The deployer is subject to obligations even when using vendor-supplied AI.

2. **Start the AI literacy programme this quarter.** It is the obligation with the February 2025 deadline, the lowest cost to execute, and the first thing an auditor checks. A documented, role-differentiated training record is table stakes before any other compliance conversation.

3. **Wire Article 12 logging into your DORA incident infrastructure.** Both require it. Different output schemas, one infrastructure layer. Revenue per employee does not improve when compliance analysts manually reconstruct decision trails from fragmented logs — automate the capture.

4. **Design human oversight as a product feature.** Article 14 requires that humans can intervene, override, and stop high-risk AI systems. In a credit decisioning workflow, that means a review queue, an override audit trail, and a documented escalation path. Design it to be used, not to satisfy an audit checklist — because it will be used, under operational pressure, when the stakes are highest.

5. **Treat December 2027 as a 12-month deadline starting now.** A 12–18 month implementation timeline from inventory to conformity leaves almost no buffer if you start in 2027. Firms that begin in Q3 2026 will enter the enforcement window with clean documentation, tested systems, and a demonstrable compliance programme. Firms that treat the Omnibus extension as a postponement will face a compressed build under enforcement conditions.

## FAQ

**What AI systems in fintech are classified as high-risk under the EU AI Act?**
Credit scoring, loan approval, AML risk profiling, fraud detection, insurance underwriting, and biometric identification are all explicitly listed under Annex III. The EBA's November 2025 report found that the majority of AI use cases at supervised institutions fall into this category — not a minority of edge systems.

**What did the Digital Omnibus change about the August 2026 deadline?**
The Omnibus deferred high-risk obligations for stand-alone Annex III systems from August 2, 2026 to December 2, 2027. For AI embedded in regulated Annex I products, the deadline moves to August 2, 2028. AI literacy (Article 4) and prohibited practice rules (Article 5) were not deferred — both applied from February 2, 2025.

**What are the fines for EU AI Act non-compliance in financial services?**
Non-compliance with high-risk AI obligations (Articles 9–14) carries fines of up to €15 million or 3% of total annual global turnover, whichever is higher. Prohibited practice violations under Article 5 carry a ceiling of €35 million or 7% of global turnover. Both thresholds apply per violation, not per firm.

**How does EU AI Act compliance intersect with DORA for fintech?**
Both require incident logging, operational resilience frameworks, and third-party ICT risk management. AI Act Article 12 record-keeping and DORA incident reporting demand overlapping infrastructure — timestamped, queryable decision logs. Building one logging layer that satisfies both frameworks' output schemas is the correct design. Building separately creates duplicated cost and audit risk.

The December 2027 deadline does not reduce the compliance burden. It adjusts the delivery window. A 12–18 month implementation timeline does not compress because the deadline moved — it starts when the work starts. The firms that begin the 4-Phase Compliance Build in Q3 2026 will finish before the deadline. The ones that treat the Omnibus extension as permission to wait will not.

The EU AI Act is not a reporting obligation layered on top of existing operations. It is an operating model requirement: documented risk management, governed data, auditable decisions, tested human oversight. Build it the same way you would build any production system — from the architecture up, not from the audit finding down.
