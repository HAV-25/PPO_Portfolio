€7.1 billion in GDPR fines were recorded in 2026 (Kiteworks, August 2026). The EU AI Act's high-risk requirements entered full application on 2 August 2026. If your system makes automated decisions about individuals, both frameworks now apply simultaneously — and violations under each can compound.

Most fintech operators treat GDPR Article 22 and the EU AI Act as separate compliance tracks. They are not. For any AI system making credit, fraud, onboarding, or pricing decisions, the obligations overlap — and the gap between them is where the legal exposure sits.

## What does GDPR Article 22 actually prohibit?

Article 22 prohibits decisions based solely on automated processing — including profiling — that produce legal or similarly significant effects on an individual, unless one of three narrow exceptions applies: contractual necessity, explicit legal permission, or explicit consent. Even when an exception applies, the data subject retains the right to human intervention, to express their view, and to contest the outcome.

The word "solely" matters. The Court of Justice of the EU confirmed in SCHUFA (Case C-634/21, December 2023) that a decision can be "solely automated" even when a human technically reviews it — if that human adds no meaningful judgment and the algorithmic output is determinative. Token human review does not satisfy Article 22.

The three conditions where automated decisions are permitted:

| Exception | Requirement | Safeguard required |
|---|---|---|
| Contractual necessity | Processing needed to fulfil a contract | Human review + right to contest |
| Legal authorisation | EU or member state law expressly allows it | Law must include suitable measures |
| Explicit consent | Freely given, specific, informed consent | Human review + right to withdraw |

## What does the EU AI Act add on top of Article 22?

The AI Act does not replace Article 22 — it layers additional obligations on top for systems classified as high-risk. Under Article 14, high-risk AI systems must enable effective human oversight: a human must be able to understand the system's outputs, identify anomalies, and intervene before consequential decisions are executed.

Under Article 86 (applicable from 2 August 2026), individuals subject to decisions by high-risk AI systems have the right to a meaningful explanation of that decision — covering the principal reasons, the data used, and the logic applied. This right exists in addition to the Article 22 right to contest.

The penalty ceiling is additive. GDPR violations attract up to €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover. EU AI Act violations for high-risk system misuse attract up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover. A single non-compliant automated decision system can draw enforcement under both simultaneously (EU AI Act Blog, August 2026).

## How did the SCHUFA ruling change what counts as an automated decision in fintech?

The SCHUFA ruling (CJEU, Case C-634/21, December 2023) established that a credit score generated by an algorithm constitutes an Article 22 automated decision when it plays a "determining role" in a downstream decision — even when a human nominally makes the final call.

For fintech operators, this ruling has immediate implications. Fraud scores, creditworthiness assessments, KYC/AML risk flags, and pricing models that feed into lending or onboarding decisions are all candidates for Article 22 scope — regardless of whether a compliance officer reviews an output before it is actioned.

The question is not whether a human is in the loop. The question is whether that human can and does exercise genuine judgment independent of the algorithmic output.

## Which financial services systems are in scope for both frameworks?

The overlap is concentrated in 4 use cases. Each qualifies as a high-risk AI system under Annex III of the EU AI Act, and each typically involves automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects under Article 22:

**Credit decisioning** — loan approvals, limit increases, credit score calculation. The SCHUFA ruling applies directly. GDPR Art 22 + AI Act Art 14 + Art 86 all apply.

**KYC and AML screening** — automated identity verification decisions, sanctions screening, suspicious activity flagging. Where a decision to reject onboarding or block a transaction is algorithm-driven, Article 22 applies.

**Fraud detection and transaction blocking** — real-time fraud scoring that triggers account blocks or payment holds. These produce immediate, significant financial effects on individuals and are high-risk under the AI Act.

**Insurance pricing and underwriting** — automated premium calculation using behavioural or demographic data. Legal effects on individuals = Article 22 scope.

## What is the Dual Trigger Test for automated decisions?

Before deploying any AI system that interacts with individual outcomes, I run a 2-question compliance design gate:

**Trigger 1:** Does the system's output produce a legal or similarly significant effect on an identifiable individual? If yes, Article 22 GDPR applies.

**Trigger 2:** Does the system fall within the EU AI Act's Annex III high-risk categories — specifically AI used in credit assessment, biometric identification, employment, essential services, or law enforcement? If yes, AI Act Articles 13, 14, and 86 apply.

If both triggers fire, the system needs a compliance design that satisfies both frameworks simultaneously — not sequentially, and not in parallel silos. The Dual Trigger Test is a design gate, not a legal opinion. Run it at the architecture stage, before the model is built. Retrofitting explainability and human oversight into a deployed system is expensive and often technically compromised.

## What I would do

**1. Map every automated output that affects individuals.** Start with credit, onboarding, fraud, and pricing. For each system, run both Dual Trigger questions. Be honest about where the algorithmic output is determinative in practice, not just in theory.

**2. Design human review that is genuinely independent.** Give reviewers access to the input data, model logic at a sufficient level of abstraction, and the ability to override without friction. Log the override rate — if it is under 1%, the review is likely a rubber stamp. That is a SCHUFA risk.

**3. Build the Article 86 explanation pipeline in n8n.** Every individual decision generates a decision record: data inputs, model version, output, what the human reviewer saw, and whether they overrode it. Store this. The right to explanation requires you to produce it on request — target 72 hours or less.

**4. Separate your Article 22 legal basis documentation by use case.** Credit decisions on contractual necessity, fraud blocking on legitimate interest with enhanced safeguards, marketing profiling on consent. Different legal bases carry different documentation and safeguard requirements. Do not apply one basis across all use cases.

**5. Run the Dual Trigger audit annually, not only at deployment.** Model drift changes what a system actually does over time. A system that passed Article 22 assessment at deployment may cross the "solely automated" threshold 18 months later as human review rates decline or model confidence thresholds are adjusted.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between GDPR Article 22 and the EU AI Act for automated decisions?**
Article 22 gives individuals the right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with significant effects, and requires a legal basis plus human review. The EU AI Act adds risk classification, transparency obligations, and an explicit right to explanation (Article 86) for high-risk systems. Both apply in parallel — compliance with one does not satisfy the other.

**Does a human reviewer always satisfy GDPR Article 22 requirements?**
No. The CJEU's SCHUFA ruling (December 2023) confirmed that human review must involve genuine independent judgment. If the reviewer consistently accepts the algorithmic output without meaningful evaluation, the system is still "solely automated" under Article 22 — regardless of the human step in the workflow.

**Which AI systems are high-risk under the EU AI Act for financial services?**
The EU AI Act's Annex III includes AI used in credit scoring and creditworthiness assessment, AI for biometric identification used in financial services, and AI used in the management of critical infrastructure. These systems are subject to Articles 13, 14, and 86 transparency and oversight requirements.

**What are the penalties for non-compliance with both GDPR Article 22 and the EU AI Act?**
GDPR violations attract fines up to €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover. EU AI Act violations for high-risk system misuse attract up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover. Enforcement under both simultaneously is legally possible — the penalty ceilings are independent and additive.
